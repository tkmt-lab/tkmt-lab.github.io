Why Did Culture Day Stop Standing Out as Sunny? — 86 Years of Weather and a "Singularity Factory"

1. The Folklore of November 3

In Japan, November 3 is a national autumn holiday known as Culture Day. Just as rows of ginkgo trees along Tokyo streets begin turning gold, the holiday arrives with a familiar piece of local lore: "Culture Day is almost always sunny." Many people in Japan can recall looking up at a crisp blue November sky and nodding in agreement that the old saying held true once again.

Meteorologists have a term for a specific calendar date that turns out unusually sunny or rainy compared with the days right before and after it: a "weather singularity" (or calendar singularity, called tokuibi in Japanese). For generations, November 3 in Tokyo has been celebrated as the classic fair-weather singularity.

Yet the air above us does not read a wall calendar. No physical mechanism in the atmosphere is known to sweep clouds away specifically on November 3. Why, then, did this single date earn such a steadfast reputation for clear skies?

2. In the Mid-20th Century, It Really Was the Champion

To test the legend against the record, we gathered 86 years of daily Tokyo weather data from 1940 through 2025 and examined the 15-day window centered on November 3—seven days before through seven days after, from October 27 to November 10. We first split those 86 years in half and looked at the first 43 years (1940–1982, roughly corresponding to Japan's Showa era).

Remarkably, during those first 43 years, November 3 really did stand head and shoulders above its neighbors. The share of years with a mostly clear sky on November 3 was 33%, far ahead of the 19% average across the surrounding 14 days and ranking 1st out of all 15 days in the window. Measured by the share of rain-free years, November 3 reached 74%, taking 2nd place out of 15. As far as mid-20th-century records go, the popular belief that Culture Day brings fair weather had genuine statistical backing.

3. A Dethroned Champion and a "Singularity Factory"

When we apply the exact same yardstick to the second 43 years (1983–2025), however, the picture changes completely. On November 3, the share of clear years is still a respectable 35%, but because the surrounding days rose across the board, November 3 slipped to 3rd place out of 15. November 3 did not grow cloudier; rather, its neighboring days caught up, so it no longer stood out. What is more, when measured by the share of rain-free years, November 3 managed only 63%—falling below the 14-day neighbor average of 68% and sinking to 10th out of 15 days. Over these 43 years, the #1 spot for clear skies went to the next day, November 4 (40%). The crown had moved next door.

Why did the mid-century champion slip back into the pack so easily? To find out, we built a "singularity factory" inside a computer. For each of the 43 years, we randomly shifted the calendar by anywhere from minus 7 to plus 7 days. This creates 43 years of synthetic weather in which seasonal progression and day-to-day weather persistence remain just like the real world, while any link to a specific calendar date is completely erased. We then had the computer scan all 365 dates and automatically flag any day that stood out sharply as clearer (or rainier) than the seven days on either side.

When we repeated this experiment 500 times, even synthetic weather with no calendar mechanism at all produced an average of 10.4 clear-sky singularities and 9.0 rainy singularities among the 365 dates of the calendar. When you draw 365 lottery tickets at once, roughly 1 day in every 37 will put on the face of a "singularity" by chance alone. In fact, when the same machine scanned actual Tokyo weather from the first 43 years, it found 6 clear singularities (including November 3) and 5 rainy ones—11 in total, slightly fewer than in the random world.

4. A Quirk of the Sky, or a Quirk of Human Naming?

Next, we checked how many of the "singularities" discovered in the first 43 years managed to survive near the top—ranking in the top 3 of their 15-day window—during the next 43 years. In our synthetic weather, about 25% of chance-born singularities, or 1 in 4, happened to stay in the top 3 in the second 43 years as well. In actual Tokyo, out of the 11 singularities from 1940–1982, only 3 clear-sky dates survived in 1983–2025 (June 23, August 28, and November 3, which barely hung on in 3rd place), while all 5 rainy dates vanished—leaving 3 out of 11 in total, or about 27%. Their survival rate is indistinguishable from a world governed by chance.

When you pick the top performer out of a large crowd by luck, their score in the next round tends to drift back toward the average—a familiar statistical phenomenon known as regression to the mean, or the winner's curse. Early November in Tokyo is naturally a season of fair autumn weather brought by migrating high-pressure systems. Among those generally pleasant autumn days, November 3 may simply have been the date that happened to pull ahead over a few mid-century decades.

A calendar year holds 365 dates. With that many opportunities, some dates are bound to stand out from their neighbors. People scoop up one of those prominent bubbles, give it a name—"a clear-sky singularity"—and etch it into seasonal tradition. If a celebrated date drifts back toward the ordinary once named, perhaps a weather singularity is less a mysterious habit of the atmosphere than a habit of our own minds, ever eager to find a story in a sea of chance and give it a name.

Data and notes

  • Data: ERA5 reanalysis from the Open-Meteo Archive API (single grid point for Tokyo: 35.69N, 139.69E; 86 years from 1940 to 2025, excluding February 29). These are ~25 km gridded reanalysis values rather than station observations at Otemachi, and underlying observations in the 1940s are sparse and coarser.
  • Sources: Weather data by Open-Meteo.com (https://open-meteo.com/), licensed under CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). The underlying data are ERA5 from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S). Contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information 2026. Neither the European Commission nor ECMWF is responsible for any use that may be made of the Copernicus information or data it contains. ERA5 reference: Hersbach, H. et al. (2020) The ERA5 global reanalysis. Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society, 146, 1999–2049. https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.3803
  • Definitions: A day with daily mean cloud cover under 30% is defined as "clear" (sunny), precipitation under 1 mm as "rain-free" (dry), and precipitation of 1 mm or more as "rainy" (these differ from the official weather-summary definitions used by the Japan Meteorological Agency, JMA).
  • November 3 performance: Within the ±7-day window (15 days from 10/27 to 11/10), the clear-day rate on November 3 was 0.33 in the first half (1940–1982; 14-neighbor average 0.19, rank 1 of 15, one-sided permutation p≈0.019) and 0.35 in the second half (1983–2025; neighbor average 0.28, rank 3, p≈0.214). The rain-free rate was 0.74 in the first half (neighbor average 0.63, rank 2, p≈0.098) and 0.63 in the second half (neighbor average 0.68, rank 10, p≈0.812). Across a wider 42-day autumn window (10/13–11/23) over the most recent 35 years (1991–2025), November 3 had a rain-free rate of 0.54, ranking 35th of 42 days (rank 1 was 11/5 at 0.89), while rank 1 for clear skies was 11/4 (0.43, also #1 in mean sunshine duration at 9.4 hours). In permutation tests across the full 86-year period, the probability that at least one day in the 42-day window reaches or exceeds November 3's full-period clear rate (0.337) is about 50% (compared with about 1.7% for a single pre-selected date).
  • Singularity factory: A calendar date was selected as a singularity in the first 43 years if it ranked 1st out of the 15 days in its ±7-day window and exceeded the 14-day neighbor average by z ≥ 2.0; it was judged to "survive" in the second 43 years if it maintained z > 0 and ranked in the top 3 of 15 (z uses an independent-year binomial approximation, with day-to-day weather persistence accounted for by comparing against the shifted-calendar null simulations).
  • Comparison with random worlds: In 500 simulations shifting each year's calendar by −7 to +7 days, an average of 10.4 clear singularities and 9.0 rainy singularities emerged among the 365 calendar dates, with a survival rate of about 25% (0.25 for clear, 0.24 for rainy). Alternative null models (300 runs each)—shifting by ±3 days, or generating synthetic Markov-chain weather that copies only the 31-day moving-average climatology and monthly day-to-day persistence—likewise yielded about 10 clear and 9 rainy singularities. Why actual Tokyo in the first half (6 clear: 1/17, 6/23, 7/28, 8/28, 10/22, 11/3; 5 rainy: 1/2, 1/30, 4/21, 9/10, 10/28) had slightly fewer singularities than the random worlds (residual variance around the 15-day moving average is 0.0025 in actual Tokyo vs. a median of 0.0028 in shifted calendars, making the actual curve slightly smoother), and what lies behind the August 28 clear singularity that stayed #1 in the second half (z = +3.2), remain open questions.
  • Unverified information and assumptions: Claims found in secondary search summaries—such as "in JMA statistics for 1991–2020, the frequency of sunny weather on November 3 in Tokyo is about 56.7% (formerly 70–80%)" (unverified in primary sources) and commonly cited lists of 8 famous singularities in Tokyo (clear: 1/16, 3/14, 6/1, 11/3; rainy: 3/30, 6/28, 7/17, 9/12; primary sources unverified)—are unverified. When those 8 dates are evaluated with our ERA5 dataset and criteria, only 11/3 qualified as a singularity in the first half, while the other 7 dates stayed within z = −1.6 to +1.8 in both halves (it is only speculation that they may have been chosen from pre-1940 observations or under different definitions). Treating 1940–1982 as the period "before the reputation was formed" and 1983–2025 as "after" is also a simplifying assumption.
