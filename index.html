Paper 1 · 2026

A two-stage classification of linear generalized Stone–Wales paths in Goldberg–Coxeter images of the dodecahedron

Question
Which cages GC(k,l)[C20], with k > l > 0, have a straight pentagon-to-pentagon path?
Findings
A remainder rule classifies the family; the smallest primitive cage with no path has 7,980 atoms.
Evidence
A Lean 4 proof, checked with Comparator against a statement written in advance.

Fullerenes are cage-shaped carbon molecules made of pentagons and hexagons, like a football (C60 is the best known). The paths studied here are straight chains of hexagons running from one pentagon to another, along which a generalized Stone–Wales rearrangement of the cage acts. For one infinite family of twisted cages built from the dodecahedron, GC(k,l)[C20] with k > l > 0, we found a simple rule based on remainders that tells exactly which cages have such a path. Among the cages that are not just enlarged copies of a smaller one, the first without such a path has 7,980 carbon atoms.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.1.0 (58124b20fa4a) · claims.json · Build and axiom checks

The question is from the list of open problems on fullerenes by A. Bille, V. Buchstaber and E. Spodarev. The classification is proved in Lean 4 and checked with Comparator against a statement written in advance.

Paper 2 · 2026

Eventual periodicity of linear generalized Stone–Wales paths in affine fullerene families with convex lattice-face decompositions

Question
As affine fullerene families grow, does the existence of a straight path repeat periodically?
Findings
Eventual periodicity for eleven catalogue families in total: ten listed here and A3 treated separately; the general geometric route includes a prose lemma.
Evidence
A formal version proved in Lean 4; a prose lemma connects the general geometric hypotheses to it.

Paper 1 settles the question for one family. This paper proves a general theorem: for every fullerene family in a broad class (cages glued from a fixed number of flat lattice pieces whose sizes grow linearly), the answer to “is there such a path?” eventually repeats in a regular cycle as the cages grow. The catalogue results cover eleven families in total: A1, A2, B2, C1, K3, E10, D12, D14, D1 and O2, plus A3 treated separately. A formal version of the general theorem is proved in Lean; a prose lemma, not checked by Lean, carries the paper’s geometric hypotheses to its formal hypotheses. Several family-specific results listed below are proved in Lean without that prose step. The proof shows that the cycle exists but in general does not compute it; for the families K3 and C1 the rule is explicit.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.10.0 (8eea5994bfeb) · claims.json · Build and axiom checks

Extends the same question to the families of J. E. Graver's catalogue, using the classical link between monadic second-order logic and finite automata. A formal version of the main theorem is proved in Lean 4; it has weaker hypotheses than the paper, and a short prose lemma in the paper, not checked by Lean, carries the paper's geometric hypotheses to it. For the families K3 and C1 the whole chain, including their closed classifications, is Lean-proved with no prose step; for O2 the same holds for the ray result and the absence of paths when both parameters are at least 2, for D14 for the ray result, and for E10 for its classification and the ray result. For the family D12 the closed classification and the ray result are also Lean-proved; two earlier computer-assisted proofs of the classification are kept as additional evidence. The D12 Lean proof was written with knowledge of those proofs, so it is not a third independent derivation.

Paper 3 · 2026

Rational two-qubit certificates for two published Bell-facet lists

Question
Do the nontrivial facets in two fixed published Bell lists admit two-qubit violations?
Findings
Two-qubit witnesses with rational numbers for every nontrivial row in lists of 49,358 and 4,327,233 rows.
Evidence
Two separately written exact verifiers accept all facet checks and all required witnesses.

A Bell inequality is a limit that every classical explanation of a two-party experiment must obey; quantum mechanics can break some of them. R. Gill asked whether every nontrivial facet inequality can be broken by quantum correlations. C. Staufenbiel published large lists of such inequalities found by polyhedral sampling. For every row of two of those lists — 49,358 inequalities with four and five measurement settings, and 4,327,233 with four and six — we check that it is a facet. For every row except one trivial positivity facet in each list, we give a two-qubit state and measurements, written with rational numbers, that break it. Two separately written exact verifiers accept every row.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (cd1e141455cf) · claims.json · Exact verification

This is finite evidence about two fixed published lists, not a solution of Gill's question; completeness of the lists and optimal quantum values are not claimed. The full certificates for the larger list are attached to the release. The repository's CI re-checks the smaller lists completely, and samples of the larger one, with both verifiers. There is no Lean proof and no human peer review.

Paper 4 · 2026

Finite-dispersion temperature corrections in stationary quartic-superluminal Hawking scattering

Question
How does quartic dispersion shift a sonic horizon’s zero-frequency Hawking temperature?
Findings
A local formula for the leading 1/Λ² shift in the stated one-dimensional, single-horizon model.
Evidence
Formal asymptotics and numerical scattering checks, including a separate code; no proved error bound.

A fluid flowing faster than sound has a "sonic horizon" that, like a black hole, should emit thermal sound waves at a Hawking temperature set by the flow's slope at the horizon. In real media short waves travel faster than long ones (dispersion), and the temperature shifts. For a one-dimensional flow with quartic dispersion and a single horizon, we give the leading shift of the zero-frequency temperature as the dispersion scale grows: it falls like 1/Λ², with a coefficient written in the first few derivatives of the flow at the horizon. The formula comes from a formal asymptotic calculation; it matches full scattering calculations, predictions recorded before the calculation for new flows, and a separately written scattering code.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (00f229f7afd3) · claims.json · Reproduction

The calculation rests on three stated assumptions that are not proved, and there is no remainder bound, so the formula is supported by numerical evidence rather than proved. There is no Lean proof and no human peer review.

Paper 5 · 2026

Global identifiability of a behavioral SIR model for every integer saturation exponent k ≥ 2

Question
Can a perfect infection curve and total population identify the behavioral SIR model’s parameters?
Findings
For every fixed integer k ≥ 2, the three rates and hidden S and R counts are identifiable for generic values.
Evidence
A prose proof, two AI readers’ partial recomputations, and exact algebra checks for k = 1–12.

In this epidemic model, the transmission rate is divided by (1 + αI)^k, so people reduce their contacts when many others are infected. B. Pant, O. Saucedo and G. Pogudin showed that for k = 1 a perfect record of the infected count I(t) does not determine the parameters, and conjectured that for k ≥ 2 it does. We prove this for every fixed integer k ≥ 2: for generic values, the infection curve and the total population determine the three rates and the hidden numbers of susceptible and recovered people. The proof gives the input–output equation in closed form and shows that the model has no hidden conserved quantity, then uses results of A. Ovchinnikov, G. Pogudin and P. Thompson.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (6691a76801a5) · claims.json · Exact checks

The proof is in prose; two AI reviewers read it and recomputed parts, and exact computer-algebra checks cover k = 1 to 12. Non-integer or unknown k and noisy data are not covered. There is no Lean proof and no human peer review.

Paper 6 · 2026

Heesch numbers and maximal coronas of small polyforms with certificates and a Lean-verified SAT encoding

Question
Can the Heesch numbers of a fixed selection of Kaplan’s shapes be certified?
Findings
All values for the selected 3,964 shapes agree with Kaplan’s.
Evidence
Checked coronas and unsatisfiability proofs, plus a Lean proof of SAT-encoding soundness.

A shape that cannot tile the whole plane may still be surrounded by complete layers (coronas) of copies of itself; its Heesch number is how many layers fit. C. S. Kaplan computed these numbers with a SAT solver for thousands of small polyominoes, polyhexes and polyiamonds, in two variants: Hc (no holes anywhere) and Hh (holes allowed in the outermost layer). For a fixed selection of 3,964 of his shapes, every value now comes with evidence anyone can check: a concrete corona for each lower bound (6,574 in all, accepted by two geometric checkers) and a proof of unsatisfiability for each upper bound (5,019, accepted by drat-trim). For the square, hexagonal and triangular grids, Lean proves a conditional statement: if the formal encoding is unsatisfiable, the corresponding corona does not exist. The 5,019 concrete unsatisfiability checks use drat-trim outside Lean; their formula files are connected to the formal encoding by a recorded finite comparison. All values agree with Kaplan's. The paper also counts the maximal coronas of 22 shapes with two separately written programs, and records a restricted search that found no polyomino with Hh = 4.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (271bc8ce223a) · claims.json · Packet verification

The unsatisfiability proofs are checked by drat-trim, outside Lean, and the match between the checked formula files and the Lean encoding is a recorded finite computation, not a Lean proof. The outer-layer corona counts have no proof certificate, and the search does not show that no polyomino has Hh = 4. Only the listed shapes are covered. No priority is claimed, and there is no human peer review. The proofs (25 GB) are not attached; the formulas regenerate byte for byte, and the CI re-checks a sample.

Paper 7 · 2026

Term-minimal realizations of nine line-type configurations in quadratic mass-action systems

Question
How few equation terms realize nine line-type configurations in two-species quadratic mass-action systems?
Findings
Within the equilibrium and nondegeneracy conditions stated below, the minima are eight component terms for seven configurations and six for two.
Evidence
Exact examples and a prose lower-bound proof; separate verification shared the definitions and mathematical preparation.

I studied nine configurations of invariant lines: straight directions preserved by the concentration flow near an equilibrium. Seven configurations need at least eight nonzero terms in the two equations, and two need at least six. Exact examples attain all nine bounds, and a proof rules out fewer terms. Compared with the best examples in the source paper, five counts decrease. A term is counted separately in each equation, even when the same monomial appears in both.

Paper (PDF) · Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (dfb36e121ec0) · Claims and scope · Checks (CI)

The result concerns two species, reactant molecularity at most two, unrestricted products, and a positive equilibrium whose entire Jacobian is zero. The shifted quadratic components must have nonzero homogeneous resultant, excluding zero components and common factors. Counts use concentration coordinates; the number of reactions is not minimized. A separate verification agreed with the minima while sharing the definitions and mathematical preparation. No Lean proof, human peer review or priority is claimed. Full-text comparison of the historical classification domains remains incomplete.

Data and tool · 2026

Rooted periodic-net identification tests (RCSR, 1-WL)

Question
How much farther must 1-WL look to distinguish selected pairs of rooted crystal-net graphs?
Findings
Across 99 selected cases, 97 have r_WL > r_min; the largest gap is 4.
Evidence
All cases checked separately from the generator; six core cases also recomputed by a separate implementation.

Crystal nets are periodic graphs that describe how the atoms or building units of a crystal are linked; the RCSR database collects them. Each test case is a pair of nets with a chosen root vertex in each. For a radius r, the ball is every vertex within r edges of the root, with all edges between them. Each case records two radii: r_min, the smallest r at which the two balls are not isomorphic, and r_WL, the smallest r at which 1-WL colour refinement, run until the colours stop changing, tells them apart. There are 99 cases. In 97 of them r_WL is larger than r_min: the balls already differ, but colour refinement does not yet see it. The largest gap is 4, for tsa V1 / tsg V3 (r_min = 4, r_WL = 8). For each case, a checker written separately from the generator rebuilds the graphs and checks a complete root-preserving isomorphism at r_min − 1 and a certificate of non-isomorphism at r_min. The six core cases were also computed by a separately written implementation, with the same values.

The tests are for people who test graph-isomorphism heuristics or the expressive power of message passing on graphs built from RCSR nets. The input is the bare graph: vertices, edges and a root. Methods that also use atomic species, coordinates, distances or angles receive different input, and these tests say nothing about them.

Repository · Version DOI · Concept DOI (all versions) · Release v1.0.0 (e42785792356) · claims.json · Checks (CI)

No RCSR data is included, because no licence or redistribution terms were found at the pinned version: the code downloads the RCSR file from a fixed commit and checks its SHA-256. Only the six core cases were checked by a separate implementation. The 99 cases are a selection, not a maximum over RCSR. Nothing is claimed about graph neural networks in general, a radius is not a number of layers, and no priority is claimed. There is no human expert review and no formal proof.

Data and tool · 2026

Lean statements from published papers

Question
What do the released Lean theorems say, and where are their proofs?
Findings
18 statements in nine files from three repositories, with links to proofs at fixed commits.
Evidence
Statement-type and metadata checks; the collection uses by sorry and does not verify proofs.

Lean writes a mathematical claim with its definitions and assumptions made explicit. This collection brings together statements from three published repositories on generalized Stone–Wales paths in fullerene graphs and on Heesch number certificates. It is for people and programs that want to inspect a theorem's assumptions, take one statement into another Lean project, or find its proof. Each statement links to a proof at a fixed public commit.

How the work is checked

Each release keeps different kinds of evidence apart, and says which applies to each claim:

Formal proofChecked by the Lean 4 kernel. Separately, the Comparator tool checks that the proof proves exactly a statement written in advance by a different agent. Anyone can re-run the build; each repository's GitHub Actions workflow rebuilds the main modules and checks their axioms on every push. Any step from the paper to the formal statement that is not in Lean is said so in the repository.
Independent derivationA second proof written by a separate team that did not see the first proof or its code. This does not by itself mean that the statement, expected result or mathematical preparation was withheld. Each work records what was shared; a separate implementation and an AI reading are different checks.
AI reviewProse read and recalculated by an AI reviewer. Not human peer review.
Finite computationExhaustive or sampled checks, with their exact ranges.

What is not yet established — for example human peer review or novelty — is stated in each repository. Corrections are listed openly.

All results, their claims, kinds of evidence and commands to re-check them are listed in one machine-readable file: catalog of results (catalog.json).

How each kind of check is done, what it establishes and what it leaves open, as a procedure others can reuse: how the work is checked.

How this research is done

The research is carried out with AI systems (OpenAI Codex and Anthropic Claude Code) working as separate teams for exploration, independent verification, literature search and formalization. I, Tatsuhiko Takimoto, direct the work, make the decisions, and am responsible for what is released, its provenance and the stated limits of verification. AI systems are not listed as authors.
